Ground motion carrying a strong velocity pulse damages structures at particular periods, so what governs a pulse's period and amplitude matters to anyone designing for near-fault sites. Two studies approach that question from opposite ends of the magnitude range — dense arrays recording moderate events, and a station network recording one of the largest sequences of the last decade.

#### ***Scope & Methods***

1. **Moderate earthquakes (Mw 6–7)**: pulse identification with the Shahi & Baker (2014) method, accounting for potential static-offset effects, across dense seismic arrays
2. **The 2023 Kahramanmaraş doublet (Mw 7.8 then Mw 7.5)**: empirical pulse identification across the near-fault network for both events
3. **Simulation**: frequency-wavenumber numerical simulations for the moderate events; a 3D dynamic rupture simulation of the doublet, compared against what the stations recorded

#### ***Key Findings***

1. Within-event variability of pulse properties is large — pulse behaviour differs substantially between stations recording the same earthquake
2. In the moderate events, identified pulses are predominantly associated with strike-slip earthquakes, and asperity location and size strongly influence pulse periods and amplitudes at nearby stations; fault dip contributes as well
3. In the doublet, pulse properties and orientations vary widely across the near-fault stations, and the dynamic rupture simulation does not fully reproduce that variability

#### ***Core Conclusion***

Directivity alone does not account for what near-fault stations record. Asperity characteristics govern the pulses generated by forward directivity in moderate events, and at Mw 7.8 local slip heterogeneities, fault geometry, and site effects — basin effects among them — are as critical as directivity itself. Treating directivity as the single controlling factor understates what a site can experience.
